If you're here, part of you already wants things to change. That matters. Shame keeps this hidden, and hiding keeps it going. Recovery is something people learn, one day at a time, with help. You don't have to figure it out alone.

## What sex addiction can look like

Sex addiction is also called compulsive sexual behavior. The World Health Organization lists compulsive sexual behaviour disorder in its ICD-11. Only a professional can diagnose you, but these signs are common:

- You keep going back to porn, affairs, apps, or other behavior after promising yourself you'd stop.
- You hide it, lie about it, or keep a second phone, account, or life.
- It takes more over time: more time, more risk, or more extreme content.
- You use it to escape stress, loneliness, shame, or boredom.
- It's hurting your relationship, your work, your money, or your health.

If several of these fit, it's worth getting support. You're not a bad person. You're stuck in a pattern, and patterns can change.

## Your first five steps

1. **Tell one safe person.** A friend, a sponsor, a pastor, or a counselor. Saying it out loud takes away some of its power.
2. **Find a group.** Free 12-step fellowships like [Sex Addicts Anonymous (SAA)](https://saa-recovery.org/) and [Sex and Love Addicts Anonymous (SLAA)](https://slaafws.org/) meet in person, by phone, and online. The Sex Addiction group sessions on BetterHelp are another option if you have a BetterHelp membership. See [Find support](index.html#support).
3. **Find a therapist** who has experience with compulsive sexual behavior. A therapist can help with what's underneath it, like trauma, anxiety, or depression.
4. **Make acting out harder.** Accountability and blocking apps put friction between you and an urge. Some people share reports with a trusted partner or sponsor. See [Apps and accountability tools](index.html#apps).
5. **Learn your triggers.** Notice what comes before an urge: a feeling, a time of day, a place, being alone with your phone. The [feelings check-in](feelings-check-in.html) and [coping skills log](coping-skills-log.html) help you see the pattern.

## The first 90 days

Many people in recovery start with a 90-day detox, also called sexual sobriety. You take a break from porn, masturbation, or the behaviors that have been hurting you, so your mind and body can reset. It isn't about giving up sex forever. It's about building healthy intimacy on a clean slate. Plan it with your therapist, and talk it through with your partner if you have one.

The [90-day tracker](90-day-tracker.html) marks each day, celebrates 30, 60, and 90, and keeps going if you slip. A slip isn't the end of recovery. Be honest about it, learn from it, and start again today.

## When an urge hits

Urges come in waves. They build, peak, and pass. Get up and change rooms, put your phone somewhere else, and reach out to someone. Ask yourself whether you're hungry, angry, lonely, or tired. The [Help now](help-now.html) page has a breathing guide and a timer to ride it out.

## If you're in a relationship

Your partner's pain is real, and their trust will take time to rebuild. Two ideas come up again and again:

- **Disclosure is better than discovery.** Telling the truth yourself, ideally with a therapist's help, does less damage than your partner finding out. When they find out on their own, they're hurt twice: by the behavior and by the hiding.
- **Bring the session home.** Take notes in your group and share them with your partner after. It shows them what you're learning and gives you both something to talk about besides the hurt.

Read [Rebuilding trust after infidelity](rebuilding-trust.html) when you're ready.

## Good places to start reading and listening

- *Out of the Shadows* and *Facing the Shadow* by Patrick Carnes, two of the most recommended books in recovery
- *The Porn Trap* by Wendy Maltz and Larry Maltz
- *Dopamine Nation* by Dr. Anna Lembke, on why pleasure-seeking can take over
- The *Porn, Betrayal, Sex and the Experts* podcast, for people in recovery, partners, and couples
