Finding out your partner has been unfaithful or hiding sexual behavior can turn your world upside down. If you feel like you're falling apart, that makes sense. You've been hurt, and you deserve care and support for yourself, not only as part of someone else's recovery.

## What you might be feeling

Many partners describe what they go through as betrayal trauma. Common reactions include:

- Shock, numbness, or feeling like life isn't real
- Trouble sleeping or eating, or a racing heart
- Replaying details, or checking phones and accounts for clues
- Anger that comes in waves, then sadness, then anger again
- Doubting your own memory and judgment ("How did I not see it?")
- Feeling ashamed, even though you did nothing wrong

These are normal reactions to a real injury. They usually ease with time and support. If you ever feel unsafe or think about hurting yourself, call or text 988 in the U.S.

## Take care of you first

- **Your body:** Try to eat, drink water, sleep, and move a little each day. When you're ready, it's okay to see a doctor about your own health.
- **Your people:** Tell at least one safe person what's going on. You don't have to carry this alone.
- **Your mind:** Late-night searching and checking usually makes the pain worse, not clearer. Write your questions down for later.
- **Right now:** If the panic is strong, the [Help now](help-now.html) page has a breathing guide and simple steps for the first hours.

## You don't have to decide right now

Stay or go? Tell people or keep it private? You don't have to answer those today. Big decisions are easier once the shock settles. Whether the relationship continues is your decision, made in your own time.

## Boundaries

A boundary is what you will do to keep yourself safe. It isn't a way to control the other person. Some examples partners use:

- "I need complete honesty from now on. If I find another lie, I'll need space."
- "I need you to be in a recovery group and seeing a therapist."
- "I won't talk about this after 10 p.m. We can talk tomorrow."
- "I need you to sleep in another room for now."

Write yours down, share them calmly, and decide ahead of time what you'll do if one is crossed.

## Getting answers

Wanting the truth is natural. Learning it in pieces over weeks can make the hurt start over each time. Many couples work with a therapist on a planned, full disclosure so you get the truth once, with support in the room. It can help to ask yourself which answers you need to make decisions, and which details might keep hurting.

## Find support for yourself

- [S-Anon](https://sanon.org/) and [COSA](https://cosa-recovery.org/) are free 12-step fellowships for people affected by someone else's sexual behavior. S-Anon also offers phone support.
- A therapist who has experience with betrayal trauma can help you heal, whether or not you stay.
- Friends and family who listen without pushing you toward a decision

## If you both decide to rebuild

Some couples do rebuild trust, slowly. It takes ongoing honesty from your partner and patience with a timeline that belongs to you. One practice that helps: ask your partner to take notes in their group and share them with you after. Read [Rebuilding trust after infidelity](rebuilding-trust.html) together when you're ready.

## Reading and listening for partners

- *Sex, Lies, and the Other Woman*
- The *Porn, Betrayal, Sex and the Experts* podcast, which speaks to partners directly
- *Joy Recovery* on YouTube, a couple who share their own story
- *The Anatomy of Trust*, a talk by Brené Brown
